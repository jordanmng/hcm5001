The Pharmaceutical Industry

The pharmaceutical is a vast and complex ecosystem. It is made up of:

  • researchers, at both university and private labs
  • pharmaceutical companies, that bring drugs to market to be sold
  • investors, who fund development for promising drugs
  • government grants, which fund most basic research
  • clinical researchers, who test new drugs in human populations
  • patients, who consume these drugs
  • physicians, who prescribe these drugs
  • policymakers and regulators, who make decisions around how drugs can be created and sold

 

 

The U.S. pharmaceutical industry is roughly 3% of US GDP, and 10% of healthcare spending. The United States develops a majority of the drugs created each year, and is also the largest consumer of prescription drugs. We spend a disproportionate amount per capita on pharmaceuticals, around $1400 per capita, while peer OECD nations spend on average half that, $700 per capita1 (interestingly, the U.S. spends a relatively small amount as a total percent of healthcare spending on pharmaceuticals, because our overall healthcare expenditure is also astronomically larger than that of OECD nations2).

 

There are many things that make drug development a unique business:

  • Research costs are extraordinarily high because of the high level of knowledge and equipment required to do basic research.
  • Development requires an extensive series of experiments and clinical trials to prove that a drug is safe and effective3.
  • Many drugs fail in development, which means that, like Hollywood and sea turtles, pharma companies rely on a few successes to generate most of their returns.
  • Because of the lengthy and expensive development process, patents protect drugs from being replicated for 20 years so that the developer makes a profit and is incentivized to continue the expensive process of developing drugs. This is where some of the tensions in drug pricing become evident.

 

 

An Example

Humira is the brand name for adalimumab, an arthritis drug. Humira received patent exclusivity in the US for many different uses and had its patent protection extended for several years beyond the original 20-year period. Under Humira's original patent, it lost exclusivity in 2016. But due to complex patent rules, it was able to retain exclusivity until 2023, and in that period earned an extra $14 billion for its parent company, AbbVie. When its patent was set to expire, Humira was already an incredibly successful and profitable drug. AbbVie's pursuit of further exclusivity seems like 1. good business sense, and 2. very obvious corporate greed. It is this “gaming of the system"4 that leaves a bad impression. 

 

Who Benefits?

Most basic research is funded by the U.S. government through grants to labs at universities. Once a promising molecule is discovered, the developers usually create a private company (however, universities can retain rights to income streams from any resulting drug that makes it to market) and begin the process of proving that the molecule is safe and effective at treating what it is supposed to treat. If they are successful, then that company (or another  company) takes the drug to market and (hopefully) makes billions of dollars. This sequence of government funding → private profit is often cited as a reason for the pharma companies to charge the U.S. consumer less: they've already helped pay for developing it! 

 

 

An aside into orphan protection

The area of pharmaceutical policy that highlights this tension between the lifesaving capacity of new treatment against the massive financial costs is “orphan drugs.” Orphan diseases are diseases that affect very small numbers of people, which means that there is little financial incentive to devote resources to finding cures to these diseases because there will be very little chance of recouping your expenses. So the government passed the Orphan Drug Act, which provides financial incentives for companies to develop drugs that can be used for these small or rare diseases. Predictably, the pharmaceutical companies try to use these rules to gain exclusivity for their drugs and then show that the drugs can help different populations, which grows their patient population and potential for profit. Humira, mentioned above, is a great example of this, as are many of the most expensive drugs on the market today. 

 

International Relations

The pharmaceutical industry sells its expensive drugs around the world, which means that pharmaceutical companies and prices have global implications. Our universities train many of the world's research scientists who then return to their homes and continue doing research. Pharmaceutical drug prices bump up against national health systems which can have implications for international relations (as demonstrated now, as Trump essentially extorts investment from pharma companies based abroad. And the pharmaceutical industry distributes large amounts of vaccines and medications, which significantly improve health for millions of people in developing nations. There are certainly cynical interpretations of the charity, but the fact remains that there would be a lot more suffering and disease in the world without these medications (and there will be more disease, given USAID's disbanding).
