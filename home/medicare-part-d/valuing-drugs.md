# How do we decide when a drug is too expensive?
This is a brief diversion into some cost-benefit analysis around prescription drugs. These are the kinds of conversations that are the brutal reality of living in a resource-constrained world, and they directly impact cost containment and quality of care for all patients, not just those on Medicare. 

### Measuring health

One difficult part of measuring how valuable a drug is determining how much it really improves someone's life. There can be a wide range of "acceptable" outcomes depending on the patient population the drug targets. If advil routinely resulted in hair loss or organ failure, it would absolutely not be available over the counter for us all to buy. But in a drug to treat an otherwise fatal cancer, those drawbacks might not deter a patient from taking the medication in the hopes that they might get better. But no two patients have the same appetite for risk, and no two patients have the same life expectencies, either. So we need a way to standardize some of these concepts around the effects of disease and health.

Enter the Health-adjusted Life Year:

QALYs are Quality-adjusted Life Years, DALYs are Disability-adjusted Life Years, and HALYs are Health-adjusted Life Years. They are health measures that attempt to describe the impact of a disease on individuals or a community. 

HALYs are an overall measure of how many years of healthy life an individual has. These are generally based on population life expectencies.

DALYs are a measure of how many years of a person's life are affected or truncated by disease.

And QALYs can be used to measure how the impact of a drug or change. If the person becomes healthier, their life gets better, or increases in quality - an effective treatment will add some number of QALYs. 

While these measures attempt to objectively measure health, they can also be taken too far. In this very utilitarian way of looking at people, people on Medicare have fewer QALYs left, and so the argument could be made that there is little economic benefit in treating them. So, it is best to take these numbers as a framework and not as fact. 

### When is it "worth it" to save a life?
Another question that these value-based discussions center on is "what is the value of a life". This is relatively easy, because HHS [has done it for us](https://aspe.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/documents/cd2a1348ea0777b1aa918089e4965b8c/standard-ria-values.pdf). They estimate that a life is worth around $13 million. This number can be used when determining the value of a treatment or intervention. For example, is it worth it to spend $100,000,000 to develop a drug to treat a disease that only affects 10 people? Probably not; there are other ways that one hundred million dollars could be spent to improve the lives of even *more* people.

But the cost-benefit analysis of new drugs is rarely that simple. For example, a new Alzheimer's drug has a pretty high chance of some pretty serious side effects, it costs a lot, and there are lots of questions about whether or not the improvements in health are relevant or noticeable to the patient. But the drugs are on the market and being prescribed, so time (and more data) will tell.
