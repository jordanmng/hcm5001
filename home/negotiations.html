Negotiating Drug Prices

This has long been a hot topic for anyone trying to think about controlling the cost of healthcare in the U.S. After the inflation reduction act was passed, a limited number of drugs were allowed to be negotiated.

To negotiate the new prices, CMS did a detailed analysis of the market.

To determine its initial offer for a maximum fair price for a selected drug, CMS will: (1) identify therapeutic alternative(s) for the selected drug; (2) determine pricing information about the therapeutic alternatives to determine the starting point for the initial offer; (3) adjust the initial offer based on information about the clinical benefit of the selected drug compared to its therapeutic alternatives; and (4) make further adjustments to the offer price as needed based on manufacturer-specific data to determine the initial offer price.

  • KFF, FAQs about the Inflation Reduction Act’s Medicare Drug Price Negotiation Program

The “manufacturer-specific data” mentioned in the last point is about research and development costs, the cost to produce the drug, prior federal funding for research of that drug, the disease that the drug treats, and other market information.

And because the financial and health impacts of these negotiations could be quite large, there are lots of caveats. They mostly revolve around future competition for a specific drug: if a drug is being considered for the negotiation list but has a competitor that is going through clinical trials or approvals that has a “high likelihood” of entering the market within two years, the drug will not be considered.

The two lists of drugs are rather different. For the 2026 list, CMS negotiated on drugs that cost billions of dollars per year and serve relatively large populations of patients, targeting diabetes and heart failure. For the 2027 list, CMS targeted drugs that serve relatively tiny patient populations and cost Medicare over one billion dollars per year. Many were granted “orphan” designations by the FDA, giving them longer patent protection and expedited review. Of course, “GLP-1 receptor agonists," like Ozempic and Wegovy, made the list: due to their extremely high costs and even higher demand, they rapidly became one of the largest costs for CMS and prompted insurance companies to restrict access or refuse to pay altogether.

One thing drugs on both lists have in common is patent protection: drugs are able to command high prices because they receive lengthy periods of exclusivity, which means that there are no competitors. With no competitors and a large development cost to justify, these drugs become extremely expensive. But even these drugs cost significantly less in other countries, so there is clearly willingness, or at least financial incentive, on the part of the drug companies to participate in these negotiations.

The range of discounts in the 2026 list is quite large. Diabetes and heart failure drugs received 66% or higher discounts, and Eliquis, which previously billed CMS for $18 billion dollars, will have its reimbursement cut by half. 

Medicare publishes extensive information about the drugs they pay for, how many patients took that drug, and how much money they paid for those drugs. It is fascinating. The drugs at the top of the list are almost household names because of how prevalent their advertising is*. Some of the drugs on the list are serving relatively small patient populations with costs of hundreds of thousands of dollars per patient, and some of them are relatively commonplace medications with lower prices, like blood thinners and insulin, serving patient populations in the millions. 

These are large changes and will save billions of dollars per year for Medicare, which is certainly a good thing, but they are only negotiating a small number of drugs relative to how many different drugs Medicare is paying for.
