New Developments

At the end of September, Trump announced that he had made a deal with Pfizer and they 1) agreed to lower prices that it charges Medicaid for many of its drugs, granting Medicaid “most favored nation” pricing, and 2) agreed to participate in a new marketplace for pharmaceuticals run by the federal government called “TrumpRx.gov."[1] This was a bombshell: none of Pfizer's products were on the list of negotiated drugs. Further, the Republican party has historically been the party in favor of continuing to allow the pharmaceutical industry to price its products without government intervention. 

There are many political reasons that this might have happened. Companies are eager to stay in the administration's good graces, and many companies and universities around the country have made otherwise uncharacteristic choices in an effort to appease any number of targeted actions by Trump. It also might simply be clear to the pharma companies that earning public goodwill is important in a time when heightened drug prices and general increasing unaffordability are hot-button topics.

More recently, AstraZeneca joined in, cutting a deal with the Trump administration and agreeing to lower prices for Medicaid and to participate in TrumpRx. They also announced a $4.5 billion dollar investment in a new manufacturing plant in Virginia. 

These announcements really do look like a new chapter for fairer drug pricing. Other drug companies, like Novartis and Bristol Myers Squibb, have announced plans to offer drugs in direct-to-consumer programs. Eli Lilly and BMS have also announced that they will change the pricing of their drugs in the U.S. to align with lower European pricing. 

 

A deeper look

Even though these announcements seem large, there's a lot of skepticism about what these deals actually mean. The TrumpRx site will be for out of pocket purchases only, and won't accept accept insurance. This means that many of the more expensive drugs, even at a steep discount, will be unaffordable for most who are looking to pay out of pocket. And these direct to consumer programs do not include the most expensive drugs, like cancer treatments.[2] 

Pfizer's announcement mentioned that drug prices would be on average 50% lower.[3] But for patients on Medicaid, they already pay only a fraction of the sticker price.

Given these facts, there does not seem to immediately be much value for patients in these deals. But these announcements are new, and there's a lot of time for things to change. 

Many of these deals also appear to be conciliatory, at best: Trump announced 100% tariffs on some drugs in September, but offered exemptions for companies that invest in manufacturing in the United States. Trump had also previously demanded lower prices from several drug companies, so a lot of this deal-making seems like pretty clear self-preservation.
