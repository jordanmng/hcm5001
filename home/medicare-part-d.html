Medicare Part D

Medicare Part D is the portion of Medicare that provides insurance for prescription drugs. It was introduced as part of the the Medicare Modernization Act (MMA) in 2003 and launched in 2006. Before Medicare, there were no federal subsidies for Medicare enrollees trying to purchase prescription drugs. The MMA changed this by allowing Medicare enrollees to get insurance coverage for prescription drugs through private insurers. The insurers would also receive subsidies from Medicare for the patients enrolled in plans through Medicare Part D.

Part D was originally designed to address the growing cost of prescription drugs for seniors. Since Medicare was created in 1965, prescription drug spending as a percent of healthcare spending had risen dramatically. This meant that seniors, often on fixed or limited incomes, were having an increasingly difficult time trying to pay for medication that they needed. But the bill that introduced it is often viewed as overly generous to the pharmaceutical industry, and was fairly controversial in . There is a clause that the federal government cannot negotiate with drug companies, which critics say is a gift to the pharmaceutical industry. 

Medicare Part D covers a wide variety of drugs, much wider than other government-administered healthcare systems, namely Medicaid and the Veterans Affairs healthcare system (the VA). The government's priority is to provide safe, cost-effective care to its patients, and it does so by prescribing medications that are proven, often using generic versions that are much cheaper than name-brand drugs. This is not necessarily the case with private insurers. Private insurers are incentivized to cover new, expensive, and name-brand drugs because if they do not, enrollees will leave for insurers that can offer these new drugs. And since Medicare Part D is essentially private insurance, many of the enrollees end up receiving expensive prescription drugs through their “private” insurance plans that the government would in other cases not cover. This means that the government ends up subsidizing the pharmaceutical purchases of some 50 million Americans (75% of Medicare enrollees have coverage through Part D), most of whom require more healthcare than the average citizen on account of their age. And so when, for example, a new drug to treat Alzheimer's is released[1], the federal government can end up subsidizing the purchase of a drug that has a high risk of complications and little evidence of improving health.
