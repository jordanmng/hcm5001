# Medicare and Prescription Drug Costs


This wiki is about the cost of prescription drugs and how prices can be managed. The wiki explores the role the [pharmaceutical industry](/home/pharmaceutical-industry) plays in the the healthcare systems and economies of America and the rest of the globe; it explores the function of [Medicare Part D](/home/medicare-part-d), how it works, and how reimbursements are changing; it discusses the way the [Inflation Reduction Act](/home/inflation-reduction-act) has and will change the current dynamic of spiraling prescription drug costs, and it delves into the late-breaking news that the [Trump administration](/home/trumprx) has announced deals with large pharmaceutical companies to sell their drugs at reduced prices. 

## Overview

Prescription drug costs have been rising steadily, frequently outpacing other medical spending and GDP growth. Certain drugs increase in cost much more than that. Patients in the U.S. often spend much more for the same drug [^1^](https://www.kff.org/health-costs/3-charts-about-drug-prices-in-the-united-states/) than their counterparts in other wealthy nations. As prices continue to rise, access to these drugs decreases. 

Medicare Part D was introduced in 2006 to alleviate this problem by subsidising prescription drug insurance for Medicare enrollees. While Part D has had the intended effect of expanding access to prescription drugs for the elderly, it has not managed to rein in drug spending. In the two decades since Part D's introduction, spending on prescription drugs continues to increase. Because the federal government is paying for these drugs, the burden of increasing costs has shifted to us, the taxpayers (of course, there's a lot of economic theory here but this is the expedient framing). And, of course, those who cannot afford insurance are forced to bear the burden of increasingly expensive medications on their own. 

One major criticism of Medicare Part D has been steady from the time the bill was introduced until now: the prohibition from negotiating prices for drugs. Medicare indirectly pays for the prescription drugs of nearly 50 million Americans, all of whom have a greater chance of needing pharmaceutical intervention than a healthy 25-year old. This large pool of valuable patients should give Medicare a strong position for negotiating down prices, which would increase access and reduce costs for enrollees and taxpayers alike, but Medicare is simply not allowed to do that.

However, that all started to change with the Inflation Reduction Act in 2022, and there is potential for even more radical overhaul with the anouncement of TrumpRX.
